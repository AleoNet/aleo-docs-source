# Background

Before diving into Aleo, it helps to understand the building blocks it's built on: blockchains, zero-knowledge proofs, and the case for privacy.

## Blockchains 101

A blockchain is a shared ledger maintained by a network of computers (called **nodes**) that no single party controls. Instead of trusting a bank or a company to keep records honest, blockchains use cryptography and consensus algorithms to let the network agree on what happened without needing to trust anyone individually.

Here's how it works at a high level:

1. A user submits a **transaction** — a signed request to transfer funds, update data, or run a program.
2. Nodes validate the transaction against the current state of the ledger.
3. Valid transactions are grouped into a **block** and appended to the chain through a consensus process.
4. Every node stores a copy of the ledger, making it extremely difficult to alter past records.

**Smart contracts** (or onchain programs) extend this idea beyond simple transfers. They let developers write arbitrary program logic that runs on the blockchain and is enforced by the network.

The result is a system where anyone can participate and verify what happened, and no single entity can censor or manipulate the outcome.

### The Transparency Tradeoff

Most blockchains achieve trust through **total transparency**. Every transaction, every balance, every contract interaction is visible to everyone. This is a powerful property for auditability, but comes with significant costs, which we'll explore below.

## Zero-Knowledge Proofs 101

A **zero-knowledge proof** (ZKP) is a cryptographic method that lets one party (the **prover**) convince another party (the **verifier**) that a statement is true without revealing any information beyond the fact that it's true.

### An Intuitive Example

Imagine you want to prove to a bouncer that you're over 21, but you don't want to show your ID (which also reveals your name, address, and exact birthday). A zero-knowledge proof is like handing the bouncer a sealed, tamper-proof certificate that mathematically guarantees that "this person is over 21" while still letting the bouncer verify that it's legitimate without learning anything else about you.

### Why This Matters for Blockchains

In a traditional blockchain, verification requires seeing the data. To confirm a transaction is valid, every node needs to see the sender, the recipient, the amount, and the contract logic.

Zero-knowledge proofs break this coupling. A prover can execute a computation offchain and produce a compact proof that the computation was done correctly. Verifiers check the proof — not the computation itself. This has two major implications:

- **Privacy**: The underlying data (inputs, outputs, balances) can remain hidden while correctness is still guaranteed.
- **Scalability**: Verifying a proof is faster and cheaper than re-executing the entire computation, regardless of how complex the original work was.

### zk-SNARKs

Aleo uses a specific type of zero-knowledge proof called a **zk-SNARK** (Zero-Knowledge Succinct Non-Interactive Argument of Knowledge):

- **Succinct** — Proofs are small and fast to verify, even for large computations.
- **Non-Interactive** — The prover generates the proof once; no back-and-forth with the verifier is needed.
- **Zero-Knowledge** — No information is revealed other than the veracity of the statement.

These properties make zk-SNARKs practical for blockchain use, where proofs need to be verified quickly by many nodes.

## Why Privacy Matters

On most blockchains today, every transaction is permanently visible to anyone. While addresses are pseudonymous, it's often straightforward to link them to real-world identities through exchange records, onchain patterns, or data leaks.

This creates real problems:

- **For individuals**: Your entire financial history is publicly visible once your address is linked to your identity.
- **For businesses**: Competitors can see your supplier payments, customer volumes, payroll, and treasury movements in real time. Businesses cannot operate with this level of exposure.
- **For DeFi users**: Visible pending transactions enable **front-running** where bots detect your trade and execute ahead of you to profit at your expense.
- **For security**: Public balances and transaction patterns make high-value wallets targets for social engineering and physical threats.

Privacy isn't about hiding wrongdoing. It's the same reason you don't publish your bank statements publicly even when you have nothing to hide. Financial privacy is a baseline requirement for both individuals and institutions to participate in an economy.

### Privacy Enables Adoption

For blockchain technology to move beyond speculation and serve real-world use cases — payroll, healthcare records, supply chain management, compliant financial systems — it needs the same privacy guarantees that traditional systems provide. Zero-knowledge proofs make this possible without sacrificing the transparency and verifiability that make blockchains valuable in the first place.
